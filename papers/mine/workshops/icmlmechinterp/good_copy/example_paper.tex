\begin{abstract}
Generative models have shown remarkable progress in a variety of domains such as protein design, but such power enables the opaque generation of hazardous proteins. In this work, we set out to illuminate the black box of RoseTTAFold3 and RFDiffusion3, popular open-weight models for protein folding and design, via a mechanistic lens. We train Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) on their diffusion-transformer activations and show that SAE-encoded representations enable more accurate virulence classification than raw activations at the right depth, improving interpretability without sacrificing probe performance. We further identify individual SAE features that fire preferentially on hazardous designs at up to AUROC $0.82$, with top-firing residues clustering in structurally meaningful loci. To our knowledge this is the first SAE trained on an all-atom diffusion model and the first feature-level virulence audit of a protein design model, paving the way towards safe and interpretable protein design.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Generative protein design has a long history in machine learning \cite{woolfson2021brief} and has reached the point where all-atom diffusion models can produce novel protein structures that bind to existing proteins and nucleic acids, speed up chemical reactions, and fold in the wet lab \cite{watson2023rfdiffusion,krishna2024rfaa,butcher2025rfdiffusion3}. These models operate at the level of individual atoms rather than amino acids, giving them a rich vocabulary for designing complex structures. The same capability makes them potentially dual-use: given a hazardous template such as the active site of a neurotoxin or a viral effector, a diffusion model can in principle scaffold new proteins around it. Existing biosecurity screens lean on sequence homology or sequence-level deep classifiers \cite{garg2008virulentpred,singh2024vfpred,sun2024dtvf,chen2025virulenthunter}. They flag a sequence after the fact but say nothing about what the model was attending to internally, and they cannot intervene during sampling.

Mechanistic interpretability offers an alternative path. The field has shown considerable promise in understanding Large Language Models \cite{cunningham2024sparse,adams2025mechanistic,bricken2023monosemanticity} and vision models \cite{joseph2025prisma,simonyan2014deep,Ben_Melech_Stan_2024_CVPR}: by decomposing intermediate activations into human-readable directions, one can read the model's internal representation of a concept and, in principle, edit it. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) \cite{bricken2023monosemanticity,cunningham2024sparse,templeton2024scaling} are the dominant tool for this decomposition, having been applied to protein language models \cite{simon2025interplm,adams2025mechanistic,parsan2025interpretable} but not yet to all-atom diffusion-based protein design models.

In this work, we train SAEs on RFdiffusion3 (RFD3) \cite{butcher2025rfdiffusion3} and its folding counterpart RoseTTAFold3 (RF3) \cite{krishna2024rfaa}, collecting residual-stream activations while the models process a length-matched dataset of virulent and benign protein sequences. We use AUROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve, ranging from $0.5$ for chance to $1.0$ for perfect class separation) as the primary metric throughout. Our goals are to determine whether SAE-encoded representations support virulence classification better than raw activations, and whether individual SAE directions correspond to structurally interpretable virulence-associated features.

The best probe on RFD3 block-12 SAE features reaches AUROC $0.877 \pm 0.025$ under random splits and $0.817 \pm 0.10$ under homology-clustered splits, beating the matched raw-activation probe by $+0.054$ AUROC under clustering. Our contribution is not a new classifier; it is a feature-level account of why the diffusion model produces hazardous outputs, accessible inside the generation loop. We will release a suite of SAEs at three depths in RFD3 and two in RF3, plus a catalogue of significantly hazard-aligned features.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{SAEs for language models.} Sparse dictionary learning over residual streams has produced large catalogues of approximately monosemantic features in transformers, from \citet{bricken2023monosemanticity,cunningham2024sparse} on small models to \citet{templeton2024scaling} on Claude 3 Sonnet. Architectural variants include Gated SAEs \cite{rajamanoharan2024gated}, JumpReLU \cite{rajamanoharan2024jumprelu}, TopK \cite{makhzani2014ksparse,gao2025scaling}, and BatchTopK \cite{bussmann2024batchtopk}, which sets the per-batch active count exactly. We train Matryoshka BatchTopK SAEs \cite{bussmann2025matryoshka}, which combine BatchTopK with nested decoders so that small prefixes carry general features and longer suffixes carry specialized ones. This nested structure maps naturally onto protein hierarchy (fold class, secondary structure, local motif) and reduces feature absorption.

\paragraph{SAEs for protein models.} \citet{simon2025interplm} train SAEs on ESM-2 \cite{lin2023evolutionaryscale} and recover thousands of features aligning with Swiss-Prot biological concepts. \citet{adams2025mechanistic} link SAE features on ESM-2 to GO terms and use them as inputs for thermostability and localization probes. \citet{parsan2025interpretable} train Matryoshka and TopK SAEs on ESM2-3B and demonstrate causal steering of ESMFold's predicted SASA. Closest in spirit is FoldSAE \cite{zarzecki2025foldsae}, which trains SAEs on the predecessor RFdiffusion \cite{watson2023rfdiffusion} and identifies mostly secondary-structure features at the token level. RFD3 shares no code with RFdiffusion: it operates on a mixed token-and-atom representation derived from RFAA \cite{krishna2024rfaa,abramson2024alphafold3}, motivating a fresh feature dictionary at the atomic level.

\paragraph{Virulence prediction.} Bacterial virulence-factor classifiers have evolved from compositional SVMs \cite{garg2008virulentpred} to alignment-aware ensembles \cite{singh2024vfpred} to PLM fine-tunes \cite{sun2024dtvf,chen2025virulenthunter}. The reported sequence-only SOTA on the standard 576/576 benchmark is DTVF \cite{sun2024dtvf} at AUROC $0.92$. These methods predict from sequence alone and offer no per-residue rationale.

\paragraph{Steering generative models.} Activation steering \cite{turner2023activationaddition,li2023inferencetime,templeton2024scaling} suggests that adding a feature direction to a residual stream during inference can shift model behaviour. \citet{parsan2025interpretable} apply this to ESMFold and shift predicted SASA by $31.5\%$. We treat steering as out of scope for this work and discuss it in \cref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Background: Sparse Autoencoders}
\label{sec:background}

Given an activation vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, an SAE is a pair of linear maps with biases,
\begin{equation}
    z = \sigma(W_e x + b_e), \qquad \hat{x} = W_d z + b_d,
\end{equation}
trained to minimize a reconstruction loss with a sparsity constraint:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_x \big[\|x - \hat{x}\|_2^2\big] + \lambda \, \mathcal{S}(z).
\end{equation}
$W_e \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$, $W_d \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times m}$ with $m \gg d$. Decoder columns are constrained to unit $L_2$ norm \cite{bricken2023monosemanticity}. The key insight motivating SAEs is that neural networks store more features than they have dimensions by placing them in superposition \cite{elhage2022superposition}; the SAE projects into a higher-dimensional sparse space to untangle these overlapping directions.

\paragraph{BatchTopK.} Instead of an $L_1$ penalty $\mathcal{S}(z) = \|z\|_1$, BatchTopK \cite{bussmann2024batchtopk} replaces $\sigma$ with a top-$BK$ mask over the entire batch of $B$ samples:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma(u) = u \odot \mathbf{1}\!\big[u \geq \tau_{B,K}(U)\big],
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{B,K}(U)$ is the $BK$-th largest entry of the pre-activation tensor $U \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times m}$. At inference $\tau$ is replaced by a learnable scalar threshold for batch independence.

\paragraph{Matryoshka.} A Matryoshka SAE \cite{bussmann2025matryoshka} partitions decoder columns into $p$ nested groups of fractional sizes $g_1 < g_1 + g_2 < \cdots = 1$ and computes one reconstruction $\hat{x}^{(i)}$ per prefix. The total loss
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{matryoshka}} = \sum_{i=1}^{p} w_i \, \big\|x - \hat{x}^{(i)}\big\|_2^2
\end{equation}
forces the first prefix to capture general structure and later prefixes to carry specific detail, mitigating feature absorption.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Models and hookpoints}

We probe two models from the foundry codebase \cite{butcher2025rfdiffusion3}. RFD3 is an all-atom diffusion model trained over a mixed token-and-atom representation: each amino acid residue is represented by backbone and sidechain atoms, and the model iteratively denoises their coordinates through an 18-block transformer. RF3 is the corresponding folding model, sharing the same internal architecture. We register PyTorch forward hooks on the EMA shadow of each model and capture residual-stream activations at the output of three transition blocks in RFD3 (blocks 6, 8, 12 of 18) and two in RF3 (blocks 12, 16). Activations have feature dimension $d = 768$ and are streamed to disk in HDF5 alongside per-design metadata.

\subsection{Datasets}

We construct a length-matched binary corpus (\textit{SafeProtein}) by drawing virulent sequences from SafeProtein \cite{fan2025safeprotein} and benign sequences from UniProt \cite{uniprot2023} with the query \texttt{NOT KW-0800 NOT KW-0843 NOT taxonomy\_id:10239 AND length:100 TO 300}, excluding toxins, virulence factors, and viral entries. Because RFD3 generation is compute-bound for long chains, we restrict to sequences of length $100\!-\!300$, yielding $n = 275$ sequences (138 hazardous, 137 benign). We also use the peptide-level ToxinPred3 corpus \cite{rathore2024toxinpred} (\textit{ToxinPred3}, $n = 1200$) for RF3 only, since RFD3's preferred input length is much longer than typical bioactive peptides.

For RFD3, each sequence's PDB structure is used as a partial-diffusion motif with $\partial t = 5$ (roughly 5\,\AA{} of noise), so the model denoises back to a structure close to the original. This simulates the activation distribution that the model would produce when designing around a hazardous template. For RF3 we simply fold the sequence.

\subsection{SAE training}

For each (model, hookpoint) pair we train a Matryoshka BatchTopK SAE with dictionary size $m = 12{,}288$ ($16\times$ expansion), $K = 80$, group fractions $(0.0625, 0.125, 0.1875, 0.25, 0.375)$, $\text{aux-}K$ alpha $0.03125$, learning rate $2.3 \times 10^{-4}$ with $1000$ warmup steps, and threshold-EMA $\beta = 0.999$. Training runs for $20{,}000$ steps with batch size $4096$ on a single L40 GPU. Training activations come from a held-aside corpus of design runs disjoint from the SafeProtein corpus. Trained SAEs explain $96.9\%$ of held-out activation variance ($L_0 = 79.9$, $\mathrm{cossim} = 0.983$), comparable to residual-stream SAEs at similar expansion in language models \cite{gao2025scaling,bussmann2025matryoshka}, with all $12{,}288$ features alive.

\subsection{Probes}

For each SAE, we obtain a per-design feature vector $\bar{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$ by mean-pooling residue-level SAE activations $z$ over tokens, averaged across the denoising steps. We also compute the matched raw-activation pool $\bar{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ at the same hookpoint. We train an $L_2$-regularized logistic regression probe to predict the hazardous label from each.

We use $5$-fold cross-validation with two split regimes. \textit{Random}: stratified random folds. \textit{Homology-clustered}: cluster all 275 sequences with \texttt{mmseqs2 easy-cluster} at $30\%$ sequence identity \cite{steinegger2017mmseqs2} and assign whole clusters to folds, preventing near-homolog leakage across train and test. As a sanity check, a length-only baseline does not exceed AUROC $0.55$ on either regime.

\subsection{Univariate feature scoring}

Beyond the probe, we want to know whether individual SAE directions correspond to specific hazard-associated structural concepts. To test this, we score every SAE feature by computing the AUROC of its per-design mean activation $\bar{z}_j$ against the binary virulent/benign label. A feature with AUROC well above (or below) $0.5$ fires systematically more often on one class, suggesting it has captured a class-discriminative structural attribute. Because we test $m = 12{,}288$ features simultaneously, we use Mann--Whitney U tests \cite{mann1947whitney} and apply Benjamini--Hochberg FDR correction \cite{benjamini1995controlling} across all features to control the false discovery rate; we report features with $q < 0.05$ as discoveries. For top-ranking features we use PyMOL to map activation magnitudes back onto individual residues of the input structures, providing a structural view of what each feature is responding to.

\subsection{SAE features beat raw activations at the right depth}

The probe results are not, on their own, surprising: large protein generative models have learned representations from which structural and functional properties can be linearly read \cite{lin2023evolutionaryscale,adams2025mechanistic}. What we find additionally is that the SAE projection adds value specifically at block 12 of RFD3. \Cref{fig:sae_vs_raw} plots the per-block AUROC delta (SAE minus raw). The gap is positive and largest at RFD3 block 12: $+0.054$ under cluster splits and $+0.014$ under random splits. At RF3 block 12 on ToxinPred3 the SAE also adds $+0.041$ under cluster splits. At all other (model, block) combinations, the SAE is neutral or slightly negative.

We attribute this depth-specificity to polysemanticity growing through the diffusion stack. By block 12, the residual stream mixes many structural concepts simultaneously, and dictionary learning untangles them in a way that gives a low-capacity logistic probe traction. At earlier blocks the activations are well-aligned enough with class structure that the SAE projection costs more than it gains. This mirrors depth-versus-feature-quality observations in language models \cite{templeton2024scaling,gao2025scaling}.

\Cref{fig:gap} shows the train-eval AUROC gap as a proxy for family memorization. RFD3 block 6 has the largest cluster-split gap ($0.22$), consistent with the early representation capturing fold-family structure that transfers well within homology clusters but poorly across them. The block-12 SAE has the smallest gap ($0.13$), supporting the view that block 12 encodes class-discriminative structure that generalizes across families.

\subsection{Individual hazard features and structural localization}

After BH correction at $q < 0.05$ over all $12{,}288$ features per dictionary, we recover hundreds of significant features per block, with counts growing from ${\sim}70$ (RFD3 block 6) to ${\sim}340$ (RFD3 block 12). The strongest individual features in RFD3 block 12 reach AUROC ${\approx}0.82$ ($q \approx 10^{-13}$). Feature quality measured this way increases with depth in RFD3, consistent with the probe results.

\Cref{fig:features} shows the four leading features in RFD3 block 12 mapped back onto actual protein structures. The proteins span different hazard categories: a viper neurotoxin, a Monkeypox virus immune-evasion factor, a plant ribosome-inactivating protein, and a mosquito salivary toxin. Two patterns are apparent. First, activation is focal rather than diffuse: each feature fires on only a handful of residues per structure, suggesting the SAE has isolated a spatially specific signal. Second, the activation pattern differs between features on the same protein (compare feature 639 and feature 491 in \cref{app:moreviz}), consistent with different features responding to distinct structural subunits.

The most interpretable case is feature 639 (AUROC $0.819$) on ammodytoxin A: all three top-firing residues (99, 104, 109) fall within a single annotated alpha-helix (positions 96--114). This is the same helix regardless of the random seed used for partial diffusion (\cref{app:moreviz}). We are cautious about over-interpreting a single example on a small dataset, but the spatial coherence is striking compared to the more scattered patterns of the other features.

\subsection{Comparison with sequence-only classifiers}

Our RFD3 block-12 SAE probe (random split, AUROC $0.877 \pm 0.025$) is competitive with published sequence-only classifiers for bacterial virulence factors. VF-Pred \cite{singh2024vfpred} and VirulentPred \cite{garg2008virulentpred} report AUROCs of $0.84$ and $0.86$, respectively, on the standard benchmark; DTVF \cite{sun2024dtvf}, the current SOTA, reaches $0.92$. Our probe does not beat the best sequence-only method and this comparison is not entirely fair: we train a simple logistic regression on generative model activations rather than a task-specific model, and our benchmark (SafeProtein, $n = 275$) differs from the standard $576/576$ bacterial VF dataset. The point is not that RFD3 activations are a better classifier; it is that a thin probe on a generative model's internal state can approach specialist performance while also localizing virulence associations to individual residues and structural subunits, which sequence-level methods cannot.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why block 12.} \citet{gao2025scaling} and \citet{templeton2024scaling} both report that middle-to-late layers carry the most useful SAE features in transformers; block 12 of 18 in RFD3 is the analogous regime. This is also where the SAE projection provides its largest gain over raw activations (\cref{fig:sae_vs_raw}), the memorization gap is smallest (\cref{fig:gap}), and the count of individually significant features peaks. The fact that RFD3's earlier blocks underperform under homology-clustering, while RF3's are stable, is consistent with RFD3 still encoding family structure at block 6: denoising is a coarse-to-fine process, and earlier blocks operate on noisier coordinates where fold-family identity may dominate the signal.

\paragraph{Steering as future work.} The natural next step is to take a hazard-aligned feature direction and apply activation addition during inference, asking whether the model can be steered away from hazardous trajectories. We ran preliminary sweeps with diff-of-means steering vectors at $\partial t = 5$ and $\partial t = 50$ but saw no effect on DTVF-scored output sequences. Reproducing \citet{parsan2025interpretable}'s steering result on a diffusion model rather than a folding model is the open problem.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (i) $n = 275$ is small; folds carry $\sim$55 designs and confidence intervals are wide. (ii) Hookpoint selection followed LLM SAE practice rather than a principled ablation. (iii) SafeProtein lacks per-category labels, preventing breakdown by toxin family. (iv) Mean-pooling over residues discards spatial information that an attention-pooled or per-residue probe could exploit. (v) The benign control draws from UniProt with negative keyword filters and may carry class imbalance not visible to length matching.

\section{Conclusion}

We trained sparse autoencoders on the residual stream of an all-atom protein diffusion model, RFdiffusion3, and on its folding counterpart RoseTTAFold3. SAE features support hazardous/benign classification at AUROC up to $0.88$ random and $0.82$ homology-clustered, beating raw activations by $+0.054$ AUROC at block 12. Individual SAE features correlate with virulence at AUROC up to $0.82$, with top-firing residues concentrating in structurally coherent loci. The result is a feature dictionary that is competitive with sequence-only classifiers and, unlike them, points at structural reasons the model is producing what it does. We see this as a starting point for runtime monitoring, steering, and interpretable audit of generative protein design models.

\section*{Software and Data}

The SAE training code, activation collectors, and probe pipeline are open-source. SAE checkpoints and a feature catalogue will be released with the camera-ready version. Code: anonymous URL during review.

\section*{Impact Statement}

This work is dual-use. The same SAE features that support hazard classification could in principle support hazard generation if used as conditioning signals. We restrict the released catalogue to feature indices and AUROCs and withhold per-residue activation maps for top hazard features. The longer-term motivation, runtime monitoring of generative protein design models, is defensive: a feature-level audit of an in-flight generation is a more fine-grained safeguard than a sequence-level post-hoc screen.

\section{Negative Result: Steering at $\partial t = 5$ and $\partial t = 50$}
\label{app:steering}

We attempted activation steering on RFD3 by adding a hazard direction (raw diff-of-means at block 12, scaled by $\alpha \in \{1, 2, 4, 8\}$) during partial-diffusion redesign of $10$ hazardous PDB inputs. We then extracted the designed sequence with ProteinMPNN-equivalent in-loop sequence sampling and scored each output with DTVF \cite{sun2024dtvf}. DTVF probabilities were essentially constant across $\alpha$ for both $\partial t = 5$ and $\partial t = 50$, suggesting either (a) the partial-diffusion regime does not allow enough denoising steps for a perturbation of this magnitude to propagate to the output, or (b) our sequence-extraction step is dominated by structural features that the steering does not change. We report the negative result here so that future steering work on diffusion-based design models has a baseline.
